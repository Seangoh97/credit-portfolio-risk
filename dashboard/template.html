Credit Portfolio Risk

A machine learning model predicts which borrowers will default. Its predictions feed a simulation of losses on a loan book, which answers three questions a lender's risk committee asks: how much will we lose, how bad could it get, and which loans should we stop making.

Loss distribution and capital

How bad could a year get? It depends on how defaults move together.

The expected loss stays the same whatever you choose here. The asset correlation decides how often bad outcomes bunch together, which sets how wide the range of possible losses is and how much capital the lender must hold. The project estimates it from 30 quarterly loan vintages; the Basel formula for retail lending gives a much higher value.

Method and limits

How the numbers were produced, and what they cannot tell you

Method

  • Default model. LightGBM on 65 inputs known when the loan was made, compared with a logistic regression and the lender's sub-grade. All three are recalibrated on 2014 loans the same way.
  • Loss if default. Share of the loan lost when it defaults, by grade, from 2012 to 2014 defaults.
  • Correlation. One-factor (Vasicek) model fitted by maximum likelihood to default counts by vintage quarter and grade.
  • Loss distribution. Monte Carlo simulation of every loan, cross-checked against a semi-analytic method and the closed-form large-portfolio formula.
  • Checks. numerical self-checks pass on every run, 62 unit tests pass, and two independent runs give byte-identical results.

Limits

  • Approved loans only. There are no outcomes for rejected applicants, so the analysis covers tightening credit, not loosening it.
  • One test year. The actual 2015 loss is a single observation, not a statistical backtest.
  • Fragile correlation estimate. Vintages overlap in time and the sample has few loans that lived through a recession.
  • Simple profit. Cash in minus cash out, with no discounting or funding and servicing costs.
  • Not regulatory capital. The Basel correlation is a one-year reference applied to lifetime losses.
Data: Lending Club accepted loans 2007 to 2018 (public Kaggle dataset). Figures are lifetime cash flows, undiscounted. Full write-up and code: GitHub
